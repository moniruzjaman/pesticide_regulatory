1. Executive Summary

This report presents a comprehensive regulatory review of pesticide management in Bangladesh, prepared for submission to the Pesticide Technical Advisory Committee (PTAC) of the Plant Protection Wing, Ministry of Agriculture. The review is built upon the PTAC Pesticide Mode-of-Action (MoA) Master Database, Revision 4.0, which consolidates the national register of 5,452 approved pesticide products with internationally harmonised resistance-management classifications issued by the Insecticide Resistance Action Committee (IRAC, v11.3), the Fungicide Resistance Action Committee (FRAC, Code List 2024) and the Herbicide Resistance Action Committee (HRAC, 2020 numeric system). The analysis maps every classified product to its biochemical mode of action, screens the register against World Health Organization and Food and Agriculture Organization criteria for highly hazardous pesticides (HHPs), and assigns each product to a Bangladesh-specific policy risk tier.

Three findings define the current state of the register. First, the register is heavily concentrated in older, resistance-prone chemistry: the four largest insecticide groups alone — organophosphates (IRAC 1B, 545 products), neonicotinoids (IRAC 4A, 489), pyrethroids (IRAC 3A, 447) and avermectins (IRAC 6, 394) — account for 34.4 percent of all registered products. Second, hazard screening flagged 1,294 products (23.7 percent) as HHP-positive, of which 608 products (11.2 percent) fall in the RED tier warranting immediate or pre-emptive ban and 686 products (12.6 percent) fall in the AMBER tier for phase-out or strict control; only 23 products currently qualify as whitelisted safer alternatives. Third, structural data gaps persist: 1,312 products (24.1 percent) carry no committee-level MoA classification, the WHO toxicity class field is unpopulated for the entire register, and pre-harvest interval (PHI) and re-entry interval (REI) data are available for only 21.4 percent of products.

The report concludes that Bangladesh's regulatory framework — anchored in the Pesticides Ordinance, 1971 and the Pesticides Rules, 1985 — provides adequate legal authority but lacks three operational instruments that international peers now treat as standard: a mandatory mode-of-action labelling requirement, a resistance-management plan tied to registration renewal, and a phase-out roadmap for RED-tier active ingredients. Eight prioritised recommendations are presented in Chapter 8, covering MoA labelling, tiered ban and phase-out implementation, biopesticide substitution incentives, digitisation of the register, and inspectorate capacity building. Implementation of the short-term measures would bring the national register into measurable alignment with the labelling and stewardship practices already mandated by Health Canada, the United States Environmental Protection Agency and the Australian Pesticides and Veterinary Medicines Authority.

2. Background and Objectives

2.1 Context: Pesticide Dependence and Emerging Resistance

Agriculture remains the backbone of Bangladesh's economy, and intensive rice-based cropping systems supported by recurrent pest pressure have made crop protection products an indispensable input for national food security. The national register analysed in this report contains 2,500 insecticide products (45.9 percent of the register), 1,052 fungicides (19.3 percent), 892 herbicides (16.4 percent) and 665 public-health pesticide products (12.2 percent), together with smaller miticide, stored-grain, biopesticide and rodenticide segments. This product mix reflects both the diversity of the country's agro-ecological challenges and the commercial reality of a registration system dominated by reformulated combinations of a comparatively small set of active ingredients.

Dependence on a narrow chemical base carries a well-documented consequence: resistance. Products built on acetylcholinesterase-inhibiting organophosphates and carbamates (IRAC groups 1B and 1A), sodium-channel-modulating pyrethroids (IRAC 3A) and β-tubulin-binding benzimidazoles (FRAC 1) are classified by their respective international action committees as high resistance-risk tools that require strict rotation strategies. At the product level, 3,319 products (60.9 percent of the register) carry a High resistance-risk rating in the source database, while a further 840 products (15.4 percent) cannot be rated at all because their mode of action is unmapped. In the absence of enforceable rotation rules, the practical effect is that farmers can and do apply the same biochemical target site repeatedly within a season, which is precisely the exposure pattern that selects for resistant pest populations.

International context reinforces the urgency of the review. Reference regulators in the European Union, the United States, Canada, Australia and elsewhere have progressively withdrawn or restricted many of the actives still circulating in South Asian markets, and global instruments such as the Stockholm Convention on persistent organic pollutants now list several of them for global elimination. The source database records that 296 registered products contain active ingredients banned in the European Union and listed in Annex A of the Stockholm Convention, and a further 268 products are built on actives that are EU-restricted with high residue concerns. Understanding how many such products remain on the national register — and which policy tier each belongs to — is the empirical foundation on which this report builds its recommendations.

2.2 Purpose and Objectives of This Report

The purpose of this report is to provide PTAC and the Plant Protection Wing with a submission-ready, evidence-based assessment of the national pesticide register and the regulatory posture it implies. The report pursues four objectives. Each objective corresponds to a chapter of the analysis and to identifiable policy instruments, so that the Committee can act on individual findings without waiting for the whole programme to complete.

  1. Characterise the composition of the national register by product type, mode-of-action group, combination status and registration holder, using a single consistent aggregation of the 5,452-product master register (Chapter 5).

  2. Quantify hazard and policy exposure by screening the register against WHO/FAO HHP criteria, EU and other reference-regulator status flags, and the Bangladesh policy risk tiers defined in the database (Chapter 6).

  3. Diagnose structural regulatory gaps — classification deficits, data-quality shortfalls and resistance-management weaknesses — that constrain effective control under the current legal framework (Chapter 7).

  4. Recommend prioritised, executable measures with responsible bodies and implementation horizons, aligned with international resistance-management practice (Chapter 8).

2.3 Scope and Limitations

The scope of this report is the registered product universe captured in Revision 4.0 of the PTAC master database, comprising 5,452 product records, 260 normalised active-ingredient dictionary entries, 1,399 combination-product mappings and 880 registration-holder profiles. The report addresses regulatory composition, hazard screening and resistance-management posture; it does not evaluate field efficacy, residue levels in food commodities, environmental fate, or the economic cost of any proposed restriction, all of which require separate technical studies. Findings are therefore framed as regulatory-risk indicators rather than as definitive risk assessments for individual products.

Three limitations should be noted when interpreting the figures. First, product counts attributed to individual mode-of-action groups exceed the number of physical products whenever a combination product contributes to more than one group, so group-level shares are reported against the full register and are explicitly labelled as such. Second, the register inherits the classification confidence of the source mapping: products matched through the active-ingredient dictionary (86.4 percent) are more reliably classified than products whose MoA string derives from the legacy register code (13.6 percent). Third, the WHO toxicity classification column exists in the database schema but is not populated for any product, so acute-toxicity conclusions in this report rest on the HHP screening layer rather than on a complete WHO class distribution.

3. Regulatory and Institutional Framework

3.1 Legal Instruments Governing Pesticides

The legal foundation of pesticide control in Bangladesh is the Pesticides Ordinance, 1971 (Ordinance No. II of 1971), which regulates the import, manufacture, formulation, sale, distribution and use of agricultural pesticides and empowers the government to prohibit unregistered products, to prescribe registration procedures and to constitute a technical advisory committee. The Ordinance is operationalised through the Pesticides Rules, 1985, framed under the Ordinance and subsequently amended, which detail the registration procedure, data requirements, dealer licensing, packaging and labelling obligations, record-keeping duties and offence provisions. Together these instruments establish the core permit architecture: no pesticide may be marketed in Bangladesh without registration, and no dealer may sell pesticides without a licence issued through the Department of Agricultural Extension field administration.

This statutory framework has proven durable, but it predates the modern resistance-management paradigm. The Ordinance and Rules regulate products as discrete chemical entities; they contain no obligation to declare a product's mode-of-action group on the label, no quantitative limits on how often a single mode of action may be applied within a season, and no mechanism that ties registration renewal to a documented resistance-management plan. The practical consequence, visible throughout the register analysis in Chapters 5 and 6, is that functionally identical chemistries enter the market under dozens of brand names without any signal to the user that they rotate the same target site. Modernising this architecture does not require a new statute: the existing rule-making powers are sufficient to introduce MoA labelling and rotation conditions through amended rules and registration conditions.

3.2 Institutional Architecture

Four institutional actors carry the pesticide regulatory mandate. The Ministry of Agriculture (MoA) holds overall policy authority and issues statutory rules and registration decisions. The Plant Protection Wing (PPW) of the Ministry serves as the administrative nucleus for pesticide regulation, including the maintenance of the register and the technical secretariat function for advisory processes. The Department of Agricultural Extension (DAE) enforces controls in the field through its national, district and upazila networks: dealer licensing, inspection of sales points, monitoring of use practices and delivery of integrated pest management training. Finally, the Pesticide Technical Advisory Committee (PTAC) — the recipient body of this report — provides the scientific evaluation layer, assessing registration applications, efficacy and toxicity evidence, and technical questions referred by the Ministry.

The institutional design concentrates analytical capacity in PTAC and enforcement capacity in DAE, which is efficient in principle but creates a structural dependency: the quality of national decisions is bounded by the quality of the technical evidence base that PTAC receives. The master database that underpins this report is a direct response to that dependency. By normalising 5,452 product records against 260 active-ingredient dictionary entries and three international classification schemes, and by logging 189 mapping corrections in its Rev 4 update trail, the database converts a fragmented product list into an auditable regulatory instrument. Sustaining that asset — through the annual re-mapping cycle recommended in Chapter 8 — is as much an institutional task as a technical one.

3.3 International Classification Alignment and Comparative Labelling Mandates

Bangladesh's classification framework in this submission rests on three international schemes. The IRAC Mode of Action Classification (v11.3) organises insecticides into numbered groups and subgroups by target site, with the subgroup letters (1A versus 1B, 3A versus 3B) treated as strategically decisive for rotation. The FRAC Code List (2024) performs the same function for fungicides, distinguishing single-site high-risk groups such as 1, 4, 7 and 11 from multi-site protectants in the M series. The HRAC classification migrated from legacy letters to numeric codes in 2020, and the database carries both forms for crosswalk purposes. Hazard screening applies the WHO/FAO criteria for highly hazardous pesticides together with reference-regulator status flags drawn from EU Regulation 1107/2008 outcomes, United States registration status and other national ban lists.

Comparative practice shows that mode-of-action transparency is now a regulatory norm rather than a voluntary stewardship measure. Health Canada (PMRA Regulatory Directive DIR2013-04) requires multi-group labelling — for example, "GROUP 1 2 3 HERBICIDES" — on all products containing multiple sites of action. The United States EPA (PR Notices 2016-X and 2017-1) directs registrants to include IRAC/FRAC/HRAC group identifiers on labels to support resistance management. The Australian APVMA makes MoA codes mandatory on labels and requires alignment with published resistance-management strategies. Bangladesh has no equivalent mandate; its registration certificates do not require MoA group declarations, and its labels therefore cannot communicate rotation guidance. Closing this gap requires only a labelling condition within existing rule-making authority, and Chapter 8 proposes exactly that as the first short-term recommendation.

Two properties of the database deserve emphasis. 4.2 Classification Standards and the Unified Notation

The database certifies its classification basis against IRAC MoA Classification v11.3 (insecticides), the FRAC Code List 2024 (fungicides) and the HRAC global classification in its 2020 numeric form (herbicides). Because modern products frequently combine several active ingredients, a unified notation standard is required to express multi-MoA products unambiguously. The standard distinguishes premixes (two or more active ingredients formulated in one product, denoted by a forward slash in the FRAC and HRAC systems) from tank mixes (separate products combined in the spray tank, denoted by a plus sign), preserves IRAC subgroup letters, and reserves the codes FRAC U, IRAC UN and HRAC Z/0 for products pending classification. The notation is more than a documentation convention: it is the hook on which quantitative application limits hang. 4.3 Analytical Method

All statistics in this report were computed by aggregating the Product_Register sheet directly, so that every figure traces to one consistent source. The aggregation treats each of the 5,452 rows as one registered product; counts by pesticide type, committee, policy tier, HHP status and mapping basis are simple tallies of the respective columns; and the top-mode-of-action ranking counts each product once under each normalised MoA code it contains, which is why combination-heavy groups appear in multiple rows. Percentage shares of MoA groups are therefore expressed against the full register of 5,452 products and are additive beyond 100 percent when summed across groups.

Policy-tier totals follow the Policy_Dashboard definitions: products containing one or more blacklist HHP actives are RED (immediate or pre-emptive ban); products containing actives under phase-out, substitution or strict-control decisions are AMBER; EU- or US-approved biopesticides and botanicals flagged as safer alternatives are GREEN; and all remaining products are STANDARD. The HHP-positive flag is a binary screen against WHO/FAO HHP criteria using the EU and global regulatory status column. Where this report cites group metadata — mechanism, resistance risk, rotation partners — it quotes the MoA_Groups and committee sheets of the database verbatim. Minor differences between counts published in the database dashboard and counts computed here (for example, 607 RED products in the register column plus one product explicitly flagged for multiple HHPs, against the dashboard total of 608) are noted in the text and do not alter any conclusion.

The national register contains 5,452 approved products across eight pesticide categories, supplied by 880 registration holders and built on 464 indexed active-ingredient common names. Classification coverage is strong but incomplete: 4,140 products (76.0 percent) are assigned to one of the three resistance committees — 2,396 to IRAC, 979 to FRAC and 765 to HRAC — while 1,312 products (24.1 percent) remain committee-unclassified. Mapping against the 260-entry active-ingredient dictionary resolves 4,711 products (86.4 percent, including the 189 records corrected in the Rev 4 AgriChem batch), 740 products are classified only through legacy register codes, and one product is unmapped. Figure 1 shows the register's composition by product type. Insecticides dominate with 2,500 products — 45.9 percent of the register — and the share rises to 46.9 percent when the 88 stored-grain insecticides are included. Fungicides (1,052 products) and herbicides (892) form the second and third blocks, reflecting rice, vegetable and wheat protection needs respectively. The public-health segment is substantial at 665 products (12.2 percent), driven largely by household insecticide formulations, while miticides account for 205 products.

Two segments warrant policy attention despite their small size. The biopesticide category holds only 35 products (0.6 percent), which is strikingly low against the 23-product GREEN whitelist of internationally approved safer alternatives and against the biopesticide growth observed in reference markets; the segment is too thin to absorb substitution demand if RED-tier products are withdrawn without transition planning. The rodenticide segment (15 products) is small but concentrates second-generation anticoagulants (bromadiolone, flocoumafen, brodifacoum per the MoA group reference), a chemistry class with significant secondary-poisoning concerns that the HHP screening layer already flags.

5.4 Combination Products

Combination products — formulations containing two or more active ingredients with distinct modes of action — number 1,398 in the register (25.6 percent) and are mapped per component in the 1,399-entry Combination Register. The combination segment is dominated by insecticide premixes: the single most repeated pattern in the register pairs the juvenile-hormone mimetic pyriproxyfen (IRAC 7C) with the pyrethroid fenpropathrin (IRAC 3A), a combination registered under at least a dozen separate brand names for crops as varied as tea, mango, brinjal, jute and cotton. Other recurring families pair organophosphates with pyrethroids (IRAC 1B + 3A) and neonicotinoids with emamectin benzoate (IRAC 4A + 6).

Combinations are double-edged from a resistance-management standpoint. Used correctly, a premix delivers two target sites simultaneously and can dilute selection pressure on each partner — the basis for FRAC's more lenient one-half application limit for high-risk premix components. Used incorrectly, they multiply exposure: when ten brands of the same 7C + 3A premix circulate, a farmer rotating brands is not rotating modes of action at all. The register-level view provided by the Combination Register — which assigns each premix a full MoA string such as IRAC 7C + 3A — is precisely the instrument needed to police this distinction, provided group codes appear on labels as recommended in Chapter 8.

Every product in the register is assigned to one of four policy risk tiers based on its active-ingredient content. The RED tier captures products containing blacklist HHP actives that warrant immediate or pre-emptive ban; the AMBER tier captures actives under phase-out, substitution or strict-control decisions; the GREEN tier marks internationally approved biopesticides and botanicals serving as safer alternatives; and the STANDARD tier covers products with no HHP flag, to which ordinary registration controls apply. 6.2 HHP Screening Against WHO/FAO Criteria and Reference Regulators

The database screens the full register against WHO/FAO highly hazardous pesticide criteria using reference-regulator status flags from the EU (Regulation 1107/2008 outcomes), the United States and other national ban lists. The screen classifies 1,294 products as HHP-positive (23.7 percent), 4,135 products as carrying no flag, and 23 products as whitelisted safer alternatives. Figure 4 breaks down the 1,317 products that carry any foreign regulatory status flag by the nature of that flag. The largest single block — 296 products — involves actives banned in the EU and listed in Annex A of the Stockholm Convention, followed by 268 products on EU-restricted, not-approved actives with high residue concern, 262 products whose EU renewal was denied, and 178 products banned in both the EU and the United States. A further 112 products rely on actives banned in at least 72 countries including Brazil and China.

The screening arithmetic is internally consistent and therefore auditable: 4,135 unflagged products plus 23 approved alternatives plus 1,294 HHP-positive products reconstructs the 5,452-product register exactly. It follows that every flagged status except explicit EU/US approval maps into the HHP-positive class. This one-to-one traceability matters procedurally: when PTAC acts on individual registrations, the tier and the foreign-status evidence for each decision are already attached to the product record, so the Committee's task is prioritisation rather than reconstruction.

6.3 Resistance Risk Structure

Figure 5 profiles the resistance-risk ratings of the mode-of-action groups present in the register, by committee. Of the 31 IRAC groups, 7 are rated High risk, 15 Medium and 7 Low, with 2 groups unclassified; of the 18 FRAC groups, 3 are High, 8 Medium and 7 Low; and of the 10 HRAC groups, 2 are High, 2 Medium and 6 Low. High-risk groups are a minority of the classification space but command the majority of the market: product-level risk ratings show 3,319 products (60.9 percent) rated High, 594 (10.9 percent) Medium, 699 (12.8 percent) Low and 840 (15.4 percent) Unknown.

Figure 5: Resistance-risk ratings of mode-of-action groups by committee (group counts)

The asymmetry between group counts and product counts is the quantified core of the resistance problem: high-risk groups hold 30 percent of classified group slots but 60.9 percent of products. The Risk Framework sheet prescribes the corresponding controls — high-risk groups limited to one-third of seasonal applications, medium-risk groups rotated without back-to-back use, low-risk multi-site and biological groups used as rotation anchors, and unknown-risk products treated as Medium until field data exist. The framework exists in the database; what is missing is its translation into label conditions and dealer-level enforcement, which Chapter 7 takes up as a diagnosis and Chapter 8 converts into recommendations.

7. Problem Diagnosis: Regulatory Gaps and Systemic Risks

7.1 Structural Over-Concentration in High-Risk Chemistry

The register's demand pattern is anchored to chemistry classes that international practice has moved away from. The four leading insecticide groups (IRAC 1B, 4A, 3A, 6) hold 34.4 percent of the register; adding carbamates (IRAC 1A, 247 products) lifts the AChE-plus-neonicotinoid-plus-pyrethroid-plus-avermectin complex to nearly 39 percent. In fungicides, the three highest-risk groups — MBCs (FRAC 1, 172 products), DMIs (FRAC 3, 274) and QoIs (FRAC 11, 95) — jointly hold 541 products, meaning that roughly half of the fungicide market (1,052 products) depends on groups for which FRAC prescribes strict seasonal proportion limits. In herbicides, the ALS-inhibitor block (HRAC 2, 255 products) is the largest single group, and the HRAC crosswalk rates legacy Group B as Very High risk.

This concentration creates three interacting exposures. Agronomically, repeated use of the same target site accelerates resistance fixation, and field failures in organophosphate and benzimidazole chemistry are among the most widely documented resistance cases globally. Commercially, dozens of brands compete inside each group — 545 organophosphate products is not 545 distinct tools — so market competition drives price down without adding rotation value. Operationally, concentration in high-risk groups makes the register fragile to external shocks: a single international reclassification or import-supply disruption would simultaneously affect hundreds of registrations, as the paraquat and carbendazim episodes in other jurisdictions illustrate.

7.2 Classification Deficit and the Unmapped Quarter

A regulatory system cannot rotate what it cannot see. In the current register, 1,312 products (24.1 percent) have no committee classification, 740 products rely on legacy register codes rather than dictionary-normalised mapping, one product is entirely unmapped, and 840 products (15.4 percent) consequently carry an Unknown resistance-risk rating. The compound code FRAC M02 / IRAC UN — sulphur-based multi-site fungicide paired with an unclassified insecticide — appears in 162 products, illustrating how pending classifications propagate through combination formulations. The database's own update log demonstrates the fix is tractable: the Rev 4 AgriChem batch resolved 189 previously unmapped records through systematic re-mapping, and the PTAC conformance checklist confirms every mapped product now carries at least one committee code.

The unmapped quarter is not randomly distributed. It clusters in public-health products (household aerosols and coils built on allethrin-class actives), older combination products whose declared composition uses percentages instead of active-ingredient names, and store-grain treatments. These are segments where user safety and residue control depend heavily on label clarity, so the classification deficit compounds rather than merely accompanies the hazard profile. Because the AI dictionary already contains 260 normalised entries with synonyms, completing the remaining 740 records is a bounded data-engineering task — the kind of exercise Rev 4 performed 189 times — rather than an open-ended research problem.

7.3 Data Quality and Label Deficiencies

Three schema-level gaps limit what the register can tell users. First, the WHO_Toxicity_Class column is present in the data model but populated for zero of the 5,452 products, so the register cannot support acute-toxicity-based handling classes at the point of sale, even though the PTAC conformance checklist lists WHO class assignment as a design requirement. Second, pre-harvest interval (PHI) and re-entry interval (REI) values exist for only 1,165 products (21.4 percent), leaving nearly four-fifths of the register without the two numeric fields that most directly drive safe-use compliance by farmers. Third, common-name normalisation is incomplete: the index contains near-duplicate entries such as d-trans allethrin and d-allethrin, aluminium and aluminum phosphide, which fragment product counts and complicate holder-level compliance analysis.

These gaps interact with the absence of a national MoA labelling mandate. A label that omits the mode-of-action group also typically omits structured rotation guidance, quantitative application windows and, in the unmapped cases, any reliable basis for resistance advice. International comparison in Chapter 3 shows the fix is administrative, not scientific: PMRA, US EPA and APVMA all publish group codes on labels under existing regulatory authority. Bangladesh's register already contains every datum needed to print those codes tomorrow — the gap is a labelling condition, not a data problem.

7.4 Resistance-Management Deficit at Field Level

Finally, the register's quantitative limits — IRAC's two-application rule per mode of action, FRAC's one-third and one-half seasonal proportion rules, HRAC's programme-risk ladder — exist in the database and in PTAC's field guide but have no enforcement vehicle. Nothing in the current registration certificate conditions a product's use on rotation; nothing on the label communicates the group; nothing in dealer licensing tests the concept. The combination market illustrates the resulting distortion: at least twelve separately registered brands of the identical pyriproxyfen + fenpropathrin premix (IRAC 7C + 3A) compete across tea, mango, brinjal, jute and cotton, so a farmer who switches brands for price is re-applying the same two target sites season after season. That pattern — brand rotation without mode-of-action rotation — is the single most consequential behaviour the current framework fails to interrupt, and it is the behaviour the recommendations in Chapter 8 are designed to change.

8. Conclusions and Recommendations

8.1 Conclusions

The analysis supports six conclusions, each traceable to the register evidence in Chapters 5 through 7 and each actionable within the existing legal framework of the Pesticides Ordinance, 1971 and the Pesticides Rules, 1985.

  1. The register is large but chemically narrow. Five thousand four hundred fifty-two products rest on 464 common names and concentrate 34.4 percent of all registrations in four high-risk insecticide groups, so brand-level competition overstates the true diversity of available modes of action.

  2. Hazard exposure is material and precisely quantified. The WHO/FAO-aligned screen flags 1,294 products (23.7 percent) as HHP-positive, with 608 products in the RED ban tier and 686 in the AMBER phase-out tier; only 23 whitelisted safer alternatives exist to absorb substitution.

  3. Classification coverage is incomplete in ways that block policy. 24.1 percent of products lack a committee MoA classification, 15.4 percent carry an Unknown resistance rating, and the FRAC M02 / IRAC UN compound code alone covers 162 products.

  4. The labelling regime predates resistance management. No MoA group code, rotation window or seasonal proportion limit appears on Bangladeshi labels, in contrast with the mandatory group labelling of Canada, the United States and Australia.

  5. Safety data fields are structurally incomplete. WHO toxicity class is unpopulated register-wide and PHI/REI coverage stands at 21.4 percent, so point-of-sale safe-use guidance cannot be systematically delivered.

  6. Reform capacity is concentrated and therefore reachable. The fifteen largest holders control 17.3 percent of products with 17 to 37 percent HHP shares and 19 to 27 MoA codes per portfolio, so consultations and label mandates reach the market efficiently through a limited number of counterparts.

8.2 Recommendations

Eight recommendations follow, sequenced by implementation horizon. The first four are short-term administrative measures executable under existing rule-making powers; the remainder build the substitution, data and institutional base for durable reform. Taken together, the eight measures would bring the national register into measurable alignment with reference-regulator practice within three years, using legal authority that already exists.
